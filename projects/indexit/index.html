IndexIt

The fine print, made simple.

A local app that answers plain-English questions about company annual reports. Every fact cites the page it came from, interpretation is labelled as interpretation, and quality is measured, not assumed.

01 · Problem

Annual reports hold the answers, but not for most readers

A company's 10-K runs to 100 pages or more of dense legal and financial language. People who are curious about a company, but aren't finance professionals, don't know where to look or which numbers matter. General chatbots answer confidently from memory, without sources, and the reader has no way to tell when they're wrong.

The user: someone like me, who finds filings dense and doesn't know what to look at. For them a confident wrong sentence is worse than a missing one, because they can't spot it. That set the quality bar for everything else.

02 · What good looks like

Accurate before complete, and always checkable

  • Every fact cites its page, and one click shows the source.
  • Facts and interpretation are separate: "What the filing says", then "What this might mean", labelled as a reading.
  • Short, but complete for the question asked, then three follow-up questions worth asking next.
  • No buy, sell or hold advice, and off-topic questions get a one-line note on what IndexIt covers.

Constraints: a laptop with 8 GB of memory, my personal Claude subscription instead of a paid API budget, and a single user: me.

03 · What I built

Ask, compare, check the source, grow the library

Claude researches each question as an agent: it decides what to search for, often several times, through an MCP server that exposes only IndexIt's own tools. Search is company-aware and latest-filing-first. Every result page arrives with its structured tables, so numbers stay tied to their column headers.

Compare ranks every company in the library on one figure, or puts two side by side, with the source page beside each number. The figures come from a key-figures table that code reads from each filing's income statement and balance sheet, so browsing it costs nothing and nothing in it is generated. The observations under each comparison are written by rules from the same table, and each one can hand off to the agent: "Ask IndexIt why".

04 · System design

How the pieces fit, and why each one

Two paths share one local store. Indexing runs once per filing, on my laptop, for free. Answering runs on every question: Claude plans its own searches, but it can reach the filings only through IndexIt's tools. A third loop, quality, measures every change before it ships.

05 · Key decisions

What I assumed, and what changed my mind

I started with a confident plan: multimodal RAG on the Claude API, with the biggest model. Most of it changed, each time because of something I found rather than a hunch. Read each row left to right.

I assumedWhat I foundSo I chose

  1. Answer format

    I assumedA correct answer is enough.

    →

    What I foundMy readers are non-experts. They can't spot a mistake, and they often don't know what to ask next.

    →

    So I choseCited facts kept apart from labelled interpretation, each source one click away, and three follow-up questions.

  2. Answering

    I assumedOne search, then an answer: classic RAG.

    →

    What I foundReal questions span companies and years ("Is NVIDIA's growth sustainable, and is AMD's?"). On one analysis question the agent made 8 searches and page reads, and the answer checked out.

    →

    So I choseAn agent that plans its own searches, as many as the question needs.

    Trade-offSlower and less predictable than one search, so I measured latency before committing.

  3. Runtime

    I assumedThe Claude API, paying per question.

    →

    What I foundI'm the only user. My subscription covers personal use, and one agent can serve both the app and the evals.

    →

    So I choseClaude Code and MCP on my subscription.

    Trade-offPersonal use only. A version for other people would move to the API with its own keys.

  4. Guardrails

    I assumedSet the agent's limits in the prompt.

    →

    What I foundOn the first run, the agent could see my other connected apps.

    →

    So I choseLimits set in configuration: no built-in tools, only IndexIt's, and the session stops if anything else appears.

    BonusHiding the extra tool definitions also cut the cost of a simple question about tenfold.

  5. Model

    I assumedThe bigger model gives more accurate answers.

    →

    What I foundOn 27 fact-checked questions, Sonnet was fully correct on 23 and Opus on 18, with 2 vs 5 serious errors. More effort made Opus worse.

    →

    So I choseSonnet 5.5: more accurate, faster and cheaper.

    Trade-offOpus covered slightly more of each answer key. For readers who can't spot mistakes, I chose accuracy over coverage.

  6. Tables

    I assumedMake every table searchable.

    →

    What I foundSearch scores didn't move: pages were already found through their text. And the evals showed the agent almost never opened full pages, where the tables are.

    →

    So I choseFind pages by text, then attach their tables to what Claude reads, so numbers arrive tied to their column headers.

  7. Search

    I assumedSmarter search: a reranker, keyword-plus-meaning search, a finance glossary.

    →

    What I foundEach moved the search score little or not at all. Across 46 eval answers, only 1 or 2 problems came from search, and a glossary wouldn't scale to 100 companies.

    →

    So I choseKeep search simple, and spend the effort on how answers are worded.

  8. Images

    I assumedAnnual reports have charts, so read them as images: multimodal RAG.

    →

    What I found12 images in the first four filings, and the one recurring chart repeats its data as a table. No eval error came from a missing image.

    →

    So I choseText and tables only. No multimodal layer.

    Trade-offCharts aren't read as pictures. That's the right tool for chart-heavy documents like investor presentations, not for 10-Ks.

  9. Comparisons

    I assumedThe agent can answer any question by searching, one company at a time.

    →

    What I found"Which company made the most profit?" took 7 searches and 25 s at 7 companies. At 100 companies it would take about 100 searches.

    →

    So I choseA key-figures table read from each statement by rules, and one lookup that ranks every company. Now 2 lookups, 14 s.

    Trade-offRules only recognise standard line names. Anything else is left out and the agent falls back to search, so a figure is missing rather than guessed.

06 · How I measured quality

An eval set I couldn't fool myself with

I built an eval set of 56 questions across 7 companies (59 across 10 once the library grew), phrased the way a curious reader would ask them, with answer keys checked against the filings. They're split into a dev set for tuning and a held-out test set, so improvements can't just be overfitting. A separate fact-checking agent verifies every claim in every answer against the filings, and each flag was reviewed and sorted by severity.

The fixes targeted the error types the evals surfaced: outside facts in interpretations, follow-up questions built on false premises, and loose summaries such as "nearly doubled" for 1.75×.

Checking the key figures

Before any answer used them, I compared every figure the rules extracted with the SEC's own structured data for the same periods: 251 of 254 match exactly, and none differ. The other 3 have no comparable tag and were checked by hand. On 9 cross-company questions, answers were fully correct on 8, with a median of 10 s. The one flag came from my tool, not the model: it showed growth inputs without units, so the answer said the units were unclear. I fixed the tool.

07 · What didn't work

Ideas the numbers talked me out of

  • Smarter search tricks (a reranker, keyword-plus-meaning search, tables as search passages) looked promising and moved the search score little or not at all. I kept search simple.
  • More effort for the bigger model made answers longer and less accurate.
  • Prompt rules have limits. Wording rules removed the errors they targeted, and new ones appeared elsewhere. About 1 answer in 10 still overstates a summary line, even when its cited facts are right.
08 · What I learned

Lessons I'll carry into every AI product

  • Evals change decisions. Without them I'd have shipped the bigger, slower, less accurate model.
  • Know your user's failure mode. For non-experts, the dangerous error is the confident summary, not the missing detail.
  • Put guardrails in configuration. Prompts guide behaviour; configuration limits it.
  • Showing sources is a product decision. One click to the source page does more for trust than any wording.
  • Pick the technique from the evidence, not the hype. I began with "multimodal RAG"; the documents and evals showed text and tables were what this user needed.
09 · If I took it further

Where it could go next

IndexIt does what I set out to build, so I've stopped here. If I picked it up again, I'd start with these:

  • A verification pass: a separate check of each draft against its sources before it's shown, aimed at the remaining overstated summaries, if evals showed the gain was worth the extra 10 seconds.
  • Graphics-heavy reports, with multimodal: investor presentations, earnings decks and sustainability reports often carry their data in charts and infographics that never appear as text. That's where reading pages as images earns its place. I'd first measure how much of their data lives only in images, then add the layer and judge it on the same evals.
